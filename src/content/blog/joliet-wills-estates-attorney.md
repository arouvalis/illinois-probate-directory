## Estate Planning: Protecting Your Family Before You Need To

Estate planning isn't just for the wealthy or elderly. If you own a home, have children, maintain retirement accounts, or simply want a say in what happens to your belongings, you need a plan. Without one, Illinois intestacy law decides who inherits your property — not you. Your spouse might not receive everything automatically, your children could inherit equal shares regardless of your actual wishes, and family members you intended to exclude might still receive a share.

### The Documents You Need

A comprehensive estate plan typically includes several documents, each serving a different purpose:

**Last Will and Testament.** States who receives your property, names an executor, and designates guardians for minor children. In Illinois, a valid will must be signed by you and witnessed by two adults who won't inherit under it.

**Revocable Living Trust.** Holds your assets during your lifetime and transfers them to beneficiaries after death without going through probate. For many Will County homeowners, a living trust provides privacy and faster distribution to heirs.

**Power of Attorney for Property.** Names someone to handle your financial matters — paying bills, managing investments, selling property — if you become incapacitated. Without it, your family may need to petition the court for guardianship, which is slower and more expensive.

**Power of Attorney for Healthcare.** Also called a healthcare proxy, this names someone to make medical decisions for you if you can't communicate. Illinois has a specific statutory form for this document.

**Living Will.** States your wishes about end-of-life care, including whether you want life-sustaining treatment if you have a terminal condition. This guides both your healthcare agent and your doctors.

### Common Will Mistakes to Avoid

Even with good intentions, people make errors that cause problems later:

**DIY wills without legal review.** Online templates seem convenient, but they often miss Illinois-specific requirements or create ambiguous language that leads to disputes. A quick legal review costs far less than a contested probate case.

**Forgetting to update beneficiaries.** Your will might say one thing, but beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance often override it. An attorney helps coordinate all these pieces so they don't conflict.

**Choosing the wrong executor.** Your executor handles paying debts, filing taxes, distributing assets, and managing court paperwork. Pick someone organized and trustworthy — not just your oldest child by default.

**Not discussing your plans.** Surprises in wills cause family rifts. Consider having honest conversations with your heirs about your intentions while you're still here to explain your reasoning.

Review your estate plan after any major life event — marriage, divorce, a new child, a significant change in assets, a move, or a change in health. Even without major changes, review every three to five years to make sure it still reflects current Illinois law.

## When a Joliet Family Needs Probate Help

Probate is the court-supervised process of validating a will, paying debts, and distributing a deceased person's assets. Even seemingly simple estates can involve unexpected complications, and several situations commonly bring families to an attorney:

**After a death in the family.** Someone needs to open the estate and begin the probate process, even for modest estates.

**When there's no will.** If your loved one died "intestate" (without a will), Illinois law determines who inherits what. An attorney can walk you through the intestate succession rules and what they mean for your family.

**When family members disagree.** Whether someone is contesting the validity of a will or arguing about how assets should be distributed, inheritance disputes often require legal intervention to resolve.

**When the estate is complex.** Multiple properties, business interests, out-of-state assets, significant debts, or tax concerns all add complications that benefit from professional guidance.

**When you want to plan ahead.** If you haven't created your own will or estate plan, working with an attorney now can save your family time, money, and stress later.

### Understanding Probate in Will County

Joliet is the county seat of Will County, and the Will County Circuit Court handles all probate matters for Joliet residents and surrounding communities like Plainfield, Bolingbrook, Romeoville, and Lockport. The court is located at the Will County Courthouse, 14 West Jefferson Street in Joliet.

Illinois probate typically takes six months to a year, though complicated estates can take longer. Here's a simplified overview of the process:

1. Someone files a petition with the court to open the estate
2. The court officially appoints an executor or administrator
3. Creditors are notified and given time to file claims
4. The executor inventories the assets and manages the estate
5. Debts and taxes are paid
6. Remaining assets are distributed to beneficiaries
7. The estate is closed

Each step has specific legal requirements and deadlines. Missing a deadline or filing incorrect paperwork can delay the process significantly.

### When Probate Becomes a Dispute

Sometimes the process doesn't go smoothly. A will contest happens when someone challenges the validity of a will or disputes how an estate is being handled. Common grounds include:

- The deceased lacked mental capacity when signing the will
- Someone exerted undue influence over the deceased
- The will wasn't properly executed according to Illinois law
- Fraud or forgery is suspected

If you're facing a will contest — whether defending it or challenging it — you'll want an attorney experienced specifically in probate litigation, not just estate planning drafting.

## Choosing the Right Attorney for Your Situation

Whether you're planning ahead or handling an estate after a loss, look for the same core things:

**Experience with Illinois probate law.** Estate planning and probate rules vary significantly by state, and even by county. You want someone who works specifically in Illinois and understands how Will County courts handle these matters day to day.

**Local knowledge.** An attorney who regularly practices in Will County courts will know the judges, clerks, and local procedures — familiarity that can genuinely speed up the process.

**Clear communication.** Legal documents are complex, but explanations shouldn't be. The best attorneys explain things in plain language and return calls promptly, especially during an already stressful time.

**Transparent fees.** Ask upfront whether the attorney charges hourly, flat fees, or a percentage of the estate. A simple will might run $300–$1,000; more complex plans or contested probate cases cost more. Understanding costs ahead of time avoids surprises.

**Relevant specialization.** An attorney who mostly handles personal injury cases isn't necessarily the right fit for a complex estate or a contested will. Look for someone who concentrates specifically in probate and estate law.
