## What Should You Know About Illinois Estate Planning and Probate Laws

Illinois has specific requirements for valid wills, including proper witnessing and signing procedures. The state also has its own probate process, which can vary depending on the size and complexity of the estate.

Illinois recognizes formal wills, and, in very limited circumstances, other forms — but the requirements are strict, and small errors can invalidate the entire document. The state also has specific rules about who can serve as an executor, how debts must be handled, and what rights surviving family members have.

If you're dealing with probate, Illinois law provides different procedures for small estates versus larger ones. Some estates may qualify for simplified procedures that can save time and money, while others require full court supervision. An experienced attorney can help you determine which process applies to your situation.

## When Will Litigation Becomes Necessary

Will disputes can arise even in the most caring families. These typically come up when there are questions about the validity of a will, concerns about undue influence, or disagreements about how the deceased's wishes should be interpreted — for example, when a family member was unexpectedly excluded from a will, when there are multiple versions of a will with conflicting terms, or when someone suspects the deceased lacked the mental capacity to make decisions when the will was created.

In Illinois, specific grounds for contesting a will include lack of testamentary capacity, undue influence, fraud, or improper execution. Signs that might indicate a problem include sudden changes to a will shortly before death, isolation of the deceased from family members, or involvement of someone who stood to benefit significantly from the changes.

Will contests in Illinois typically begin with filing a petition in the probate court where the estate is being administered, and generally must be filed within six months of the will being admitted to probate — making time a critical factor. The discovery phase allows both sides to gather evidence, which might include medical records, witness testimony, or expert opinions about the deceased's mental state. Many disputes are resolved through settlement or mediation before reaching trial.
